Reflection Paper

Daniela Castiblanco Diaz
Dr. Howland
ENGL 191
16 May 2024

Social factors strongly influence a person’s beliefs, behaviors, and overall decisions. This influence primarily came from my grandmother’s poems, my mother’s professional career, my overall family’s interests, and the way my parents raised me. The impact of my surroundings unconsciously built a feeling of pure love for English, which increased every year after exploring the world through the lenses of diverse individuals. This reflection explores how my English major, including the main class: the Introduction to the English Major class (191), has actively contributed to my personal, academic, and professional life.

Growing up with a naturally gifted poet, who used to recite her poems with a magical and sweet voice, fostered the foundation for my own love for language due to its capacity to create stories that transcend any society and any historical era. The verses of her poems involved profound emotions that demonstrated to me the power of language to convey a person’s perception and feelings about the world. Through my grandmother’s writing, I gained awareness of family dynamics, societal needs, and the transformative potential of literature. Her poetry directly impacted my mother’s professional decision.

The love for language transcended to my mother’s career. My grandmother also used to write poems that included themes of social justice, diversity, and peace. My mother witnessed the power of these words, recognizing how they are the path to action and to help others. This influence developed a strong advocacy in my mother for ensuring equal rights for everyone. Dedicated to Civic law for her travel agency, she reflects many of her mother’s ideals of transforming society. My mother shows how reading, writing, and speaking became her strongest skills after being raised by an amateur poet. She introduced me to influential thinkers such as Rousseau, Kelsen, and Machiavelli, whose works navigate social and justice themes for improving the human condition—topics that still influence my interests for research in my English and philosophical studies.

Unlike many families that avoid speaking about controversial topics due to the potential for discussions and disagreements, my grandparents embraced them as a way to foster critical thinking in my mother and uncles. Sundays were for family traditions, dedicated to cooking together and enjoying the meal. While eating, debates about novels and philosophy as well as their writing style, and their general impact on society, resounded at the dining table. My uncle Cesar, a psychologist with a minor in philosophy, has also had a strong influence on my decision to major in English and minor in philosophy. He introduced me to Sophia’s World, a children’s book that explores the history of philosophy through the narrative of a young girl’s life. This book is one of the reasons I love interconnecting fields of study such as English and Philosophy. Moreover, he constantly exposed me to books that intertwine a variety of subjects as well as a variety of influences from his parents, demonstrating two main things. The first one is how social factors have a strong power over people’s mindsets and decisions. The second one is that these encounters with my family members cultivated a deep love for humanities that continues to grow with every new book and every class at the University of Wisconsin-Superior.

My mother’s family influenced the way she and my father raised me. My parents used to take me to the bookstore in my neighborhood to help me find my interests. There, I would spend hours at the bookstore looking at the books’ covers to decide which one looked more interesting to me according to the illustrations or exciting titles. Having this tradition of going every weekend to the bookstore to find a new book, thrive my love for reading new and different stories. I remember I bought children’s books until I was 10 years old. The change happened during FILBO (International Book Fair of Bogota, Colombia), when I found Les Miserables. After reading the back cover copy, I found the themes that would become my favorites: social justice and the struggle for social equality. After purchasing the books, I used to spend the entire afternoon at the library reading and finding myself through the writer’s words. Although I did not understand my difficult books such as Les Miserables, The Divine Comedy, and The Social Contract, I wanted to embark on a journey of discovery and growth. These moments of endless fascination for literature and research marked my childhood and adolescence, and are now impacting my bachelor’s degree.

These external and unconscious influences helped me understand myself, my interests, and what I wanted to do for my professional life. Selecting English as one of my majors is an eye-opener to understanding the world through multidisciplinary branches including all humanities. Literature, I believe, is a magic subject that allows me to understand the writers’ background, psychology, beliefs, and overall truth. It has also the power to make me perceive the paradoxes of society’s economic progress juxtaposed against the backdrop of historical atrocities that have happened in all eras, highlighting the need for majors that cultivate critical thinking, strong and convincing writing, and public speaking skills. All skills needed for a transformative society.

Enrolling in the Introduction to the English Major (ENGL 191) was a great contributor to my academic journey. My academic advisor recommended take this class to have greater networking and have firsthand encounters with the structure of the major, the required classes, the availability of classes, ways of involving on campus with the English major, potential internships, career paths after graduating, and the process of applying for graduate school and jobs. While this class was not offered before, I wish I had the opportunity to have this class experience during my first semester of college. The readings, particularly those highlighting professional opportunities for English majors/minors, made me aware of the diverse paths available, including the statistics of English students who succeeded in Law school.

Beyond providing fundamental academic information, ENGL 191 contributed to my professional and personal growth, creating connections and opportunities besides the classroom. The interview assignment offered me more insights into instructors’ personal and professional lives and their experiences with academia. This interview helped me connect with professors on a more personal level. Additionally, the Link Center presentations of their Career helped me prepare for my internship interviews I had in May 2024 with the prestigious District of Columbia. The presentation with Ilsa Hoeschen also opened a new door for me. I applied for S.U.R.F. (Summer Undergraduate Research Program) to do research on the exploration of the unconscious through the analysis of Jean-Jacque Rousseau’s The Social Contract from a psychoanalytical and feminist perspective.

Thinking about how English majors can receive more support from the department, I envision the revitalization of the English club through constant recruitment events from the English department that explain the benefits of involving in school organizations. Moreover, placing more funding on scholarships can empower this field and thus increase interest among students. Lastly, having more classes like ENGL 191 that integrate multidisciplinary collaborations, shows how versatile English is and how it can be combined with other majors/minors according to the students’ personal and professional goals. In this way, more students would be aware of the broad horizons and opportunities this field offers.

Finally, my English major has been transformative. It has contributed to me personally and professionally. I have found my goal to not leave anyone behind by advocating for those who have been forgotten through my public speaking and writing skills.
