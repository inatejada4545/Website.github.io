The Religion of Life: Experiences and Encounters with Multifaceted Spiritual Realms


Daniela Castiblanco Diaz


Dr. Fezzey


ENGL 409


20 December 2023


Sometimes words are insufficient to comprehend the abstractness of life; however, human beings are able to create a path of wisdom, consciousness, and finally an understanding of the spiritual interconnection of cultures and religions through experiences. Phillis Wheatley, the author of “Poems on Various Subjects” conveys the reconciliation and integration of African roots and mythological themes as well as the American culture and thus Christianity. In this compilation of poems, she demonstrates how societies have adopted the wrong idea of culture and religion instead of the harmony between both. Phillis Wheatley’s religion is an integration of her life experiences that vary from her early life encounters with her African culture and Islam to her life in America with Christianity and with her encounters with nature and spirituality. Even though Phillis Wheatley’s poems have been analyzed from a Christian perspective, this paper will provide a different interpretation based on the Islamic religion regarding the poems “On Being Brought from Africa to America” and “A Funeral Poem on the Death of C. E. an Infant of Twelve Months.” This analysis seeks to show the potential historical between Phillis Wheatley and Islam through the examination of metaphors, irony, and symbolism in the aforementioned poems.


In order to provide an accurate analysis of Islam through the eighteenth-century author Phillis Wheatley, I will juxtapose the themes studied, such as religion, slavery, reconciliation of the past and present, and the first African Americans; as such, I am building on previous scholars’ work to provide a stronger literary analysis from two different poems that are not necessarily or explicitly related to Islam. Specifically, I will incorporate the works of Adeeko Adeleke, Race Capet, Vincent Carretta, Will Harris, and David Waldstreicher. Adekeke navigates the unspoken themes of slavery and oppression in Africa and America. He explains that authors of African descent, such as Phillis Wheatley, share a commonality: they deal with matters of identity and reconciliation of their past and present. Capet provides a historical analysis that focuses on the connection between Muslims and enslaved people during the American colonial period. Capet also argues how Roman Catholicism was imposed on enslaved people once they were brought to America. Carretta presents primary resources, historical records, ethnographies, and an accurate biography of Wheatley. He acknowledges the complexities of identity in the context of slavery as well as Wheatley’s African roots and possible religious beliefs in the Fulani region (the tribe from which Wheatley is considered to come). Harris analyzes how Wheatley had a previous encounter with Arabic transcripts and thus the Fulani language, which makes a strong connection with Islam. Harris covers historical records such as testimonies from Muslim enslaved people that demonstrate how Wheatley could be historically connected with Islam. Lastly, Waldstreicher presents a historical and literary biography where some of her forgotten poems are also provided. These forgotten poems examine the correlation between slavery and the American Revolution, showing a newer and stronger perspective from a person of African descent. Now that I have established a review of the scholarship of the topics, I will start my analysis of how Islam is portrayed in “On Being Brought from Africa to America.”


Phillis Wheatley develops a poem that transmits vividly the transatlantic journey and the experience with slavery. “On Being Brought from Africa to America” is more than 8 lines, it is a story that makes Wheatley the founding mother of African American literature. It involves themes of religious faith, slavery, and equality. Considering what Waldstreicher says, “During the time of Wheatley’s enslavement, a full third of captives from Senegambia were children according to their European buyers’ definition;” (17) Islam came along with slavery, the colonial era, and most likely Wheatley. Historical evidences from other scholars also provide an insight of slavery, America’s Muslim Heritage, and a new hypothesis: Phillis Wheatley and her writings are deeply connected to Islam. Capet states,


The hulls of slavers from all over Europe carried Muslims by the thousands from their homes in Africa to the shores of America. This is how virtually all of the Muslims in the British colonies came. How many were there? It is now impossible to know for sure. Whatever their numbers, the Muslim slaves themselves were significant. Muslim societies have historically been inclined to promote education generally, and literacy specifically. The Muslim slaves who came to the Americas frequently distinguished themselves favorably from the unlettered mass (552).


This statement reiterates the fact that some African enslaved people were in certain degree Muslims. It demonstrates that many enslaves had a previous religion before coming to America—a religion that perhaps did not transcend due to the lack of freedom of speech and fear of showing a different religion than Christianity. These lines also show how Muslim enslaved people were outstanding for their high intellect and fast-learning skills. This aspect is strongly correlated to Phillis Wheatley’s memory and fast development, that even though she was seven years old, she still remembered how to write in Arabic. According to Waldstreicher,


Wheatley could have been born into a Muslim family, or traded into one, and as a result have been literate (in Arabic) before being taken from her first or subsequent home. That would certainly explain the marks she made on the Wheatley’s walls and her quick appreciation of the possibilities of learning (18).


This statement provides a new evidence of Wheatley’s relationship with Islam. After being brought from Africa to America, Wheatley wrote something in Arabic on the walls. It is possible that she knew how to write Arabic without having encounters with Islam. However, Carretta, one scholar who provides primary historical resources proves that Wheatley comes from the Senegambia region and thus that she herself could encounter Islam in her first years of life, before her journey with slavery began. Carretta says,


Timothy Fitch was a wealthy merchant who owned several vessels including his brig, Phillis. On 8 November 1760, Fitch ordered his employee Peter Gwinn, commander of “my Brigg Phillis,” to go with his eight-man crew first to ‘Sinigall,’ (Senegal) on the west coast of Africa. There he was to ‘purchase 100 Or 110 Prime Slaves’ in exchange for 2,640 gallons of rum and other goods. However, Fitch was greatly disappointed by this voyage that brought Phillis Wheatley from Africa to America. It was relative disaster in its length, mortality rate, and cargo (7).


This historical proof shows how the Senegambia region was the place for control of the slave trade and thus the primary source of enslave people for Britain. Europeans justified slavery as a fundamental system for their economy and social satisfaction with goods such as sugar and tobacco. Moreover, Harris provides more information that connects Wheatley with Islam. Harris says,


In the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, the Senegambia region was largely defined in terms of an Islamic field of influence shared through the interaction of three primary tribal groups—the Fulani, the Wolof, and the Mandingos—and included the modern nations of Guinea and Mali (2).


These lines support the argument of Wheatley’s advance cognitive development. Connecting Waldstreicher, Capet, Carretta, and Harris, all scholars have proved that Wheatley comes from the Senegambia region, specifically the Fulani tribe that had Islamic influence and had Arabic as one of their main contributions.


All of these historical proofs of Wheatley’s close connection to Islam in her early life are most likely to be also connected to her writings, beliefs, and new encounters in America. Hence, “On Being Brought from Africa to America” could be analyzed from Muslim lens. Wheatley says,


’Twas mercy brought me from my Pagan land,
Taught my benighted soul to understand
That there’s a God, that there’s a Saviour too:
Once I redemption neither sought nor knew,
Some view our sableace with scornful eye,
“Their colour is a diabolic die.”
Remember, Christians, Negroes, black as Cain,
May be refin’d, and join th’ angelic train. (Wheatley)


Wheatley implements metaphors in order to portray the concept of Fatiha, specifically Fatiha No. 5. According to Harris, Fatiha No. 5 (Chapter 1, Verse 5), signifies “Guide us (O Lord) to the path that is straight” (5). In this poem, Fatiha can be seen as Wheatley’s connection and intimacy with Islam after perceiving the double standards of Christianity and thus functions as a way of resistance to the imposition of Christianity. Harris says, “In many African American Muslim slave writings, the Fatiha serves as a site of resistance” (5). Fatiha is discerned when Wheatley says, “’Twas mercy brought me from my Pagan land / Taught my benighted soul to understand / That there’s a God, that there’s a Saviour too” (Wheatley). These lines demonstrate that whereas the Senegambia region —region that is well-believed she comes from—could be ‘pagan’ for Christians, at least is a place that follows ethical righteousness, is harmonized with moral integrity, and is devoid of dehumanization. These lines also show how after her early experiences in Africa; traumatic encounters in her journey from Africa to America; and realization of Christian practices by the slave traders, she is finally guided to the straight path, that in this case, is Fatiha No. 5. Along with metaphors that represent her close connection with the concept of Fatiha, Wheatley makes a social commentary on Christians involved in the slave trade.


Wheatley implements irony throughout “On Being Brought from Africa to America,” to underline the discrepancies and hypocritical behaviors Christians had over slavery. As Adeleke states, “Her identity in ‘On Being Brought from Africa’ is that of an African pagan seeking asylum in a hypocritically Christian country” (15). Whereas John Wheatley and Susana (Wheatley’s owners) were devoted Christians and introduced Christianity to Wheatley, Wheatley perceives it as a contradictory religion due to the inhumane treatment of slavery. According to Carretta and the letter Considerations on Slavery-In a Letter to a Friend, “Christian traders enslaved more than twelve million Africans bound for the Americas between 1492 and 1870” (2). This suggests that whereas Christianity was imposed into Wheatley’s life, she could see Christianity as one main contributor to slavery, trauma, and many generations of discrimination. Wheatley says, “Once I redemption neither sought nor knew/ Some view our sable race with scornful eye / ‘Their colour is a diabolic die’” (Wheatley). In these lines, Wheatley uses a stronger tone in which she underlines how after her Islamic awakening, she discerned the contradictions between Christian teachings and practicality among every member of society. Wheatley conveys how Christian people who were involved with slavery saw African people as inferior and even as less “human” than them. She also challenges Christian racist beliefs by illustrating these racist ideas through imagery and symbolism. Wheatley states, “Their colour is a diabolic die.” She is alluding to their skin color which was associated with the devil, even though Christianity states that every human being must be accepted no matter the nationality, race, or beliefs. This also means that Christians traders did not appreciate Black people or provide them with the human rights they deserved. Finally, Wheatley says, “Remember, Christians, Negroes, black as Cain, May be refin’d, and join th’ angelic train,” in order to acknowledge three main aspects. The first one is to highlight that all races should be valued in the same manner, taking into account the story of Cain and Abel that is in the Bible and the Quran. The second one is to show how the story of Cain and Abel is the same in both religions as a way to compare that theoretically, the purest version of religions could be similar, but in practicality, they vary. Lastly, the line “May be refin’d, and join th’ angelic train,” conveys that regardless the race, background, or heritage, anyone who undergoes refinement and thus practices ethics and morality could join the angelic train, which could be understood as Heaven. These concepts of slavery and faith are also explored in other of Wheatley’s poems that could be also analyzed and correlated with Islam.


Wheatley writes “A Funeral Poem on the Death of C. E. an Infant of Twelve Months,” where she illustrates the parent’s plaintive grief after the death of their child as a manner to represent the pain and sacrifice African people have experienced during slavery and their journey for freedom. This poem emphasizes the emotional toll that slavery has taken on generations of African people. She conveys a sense of helplessness in the face of the agony enslaved people have to face. In the poem, the parents’ sorrow becomes an emotional representation of the greater fight for liberty and justice, reflecting the fortitude and tenacity of the African people who faced inconceivable suffering. Throughout the poem, Wheatley highlights how in Heaven, enslaved people, including Muslims, enjoy endless possibilities and opportunities that awaited from the inhumane conditions of slavery. Within this, Wheatley is conveying that the theme of death is experienced by every human being no matter their race or background. Hence, she believes that all races live the same basic situations such as loss and death, and along with that, she leaves a message that there is only one race: humans. Wheatley says,


THROUGH airy roads he wings his instant flight
To purer regions of celestial light;
Enlarg’d he sees unnumber’d systems roll,
Beneath him sees the universal whole,
Planets on planets run their destin’d round,
And circling wonders fill the vast profound.
Th’ ethereal now, and now th’ empyreal skies
With growing splendors strike his wond’ring eyes:
The angels view him with delight unknown,
Press his soft hand, and seat him on his throne;
Then smilling thus: “To this divine abode,
“The seat of saints, of seraphs, and of God,
“Thrice welcome thou.” The raptur’d babe replies,
“Thanks to my God, who snatch’d me to the skies,
“E’er vice triumphant had possess’d my heart,
“E’er yet the tempter had beguil d my heart,
“E’er yet on sin’s base actions I was bent,
“E’er yet I knew temptation’s dire intent;
“E’er yet the lash for horrid crimes I felt,
“E’er vanity had led my way to guilt,
“But, soon arriv’d at my celestial goal,
“Full glories rush on my expanding soul.”
Joyful he spoke: exulting cherubs round
Clapt their glad wings, the heav’nly vaults resound.
Say, parents, why this unavailing moan?
Why heave your pensive bosoms with the groan?
To Charles, the happy subject of my song,
A brighter world, and nobler strains belong.
Say would you tear him from the realms above
By thoughtless wishes, and prepost’rous love?
Doth his felicity increase your pain?
Or could you welcome to this world again
The heir of bliss? with a superior air
Methinks he answers with a smile severe,
“Thrones and dominions cannot tempt me there.”
But still you cry, “Can we the sigh forbear,
“And still and still must we not pour the tear?
“Our only hope, more dear than vital breath,
“Twelve moons revolv’d, becomes the prey of death;
“Delightful infant, nightly visions give
“Thee to our arms, and we with joy receive,
“We fain would clasp the Phantom to our breast,
“The Phantom flies, and leaves the soul unblest.”
To yon bright regions let your faith ascend,
Prepare to join your dearest infant friend
In pleasures without measure, without end. (Wheatley)

Wheatley implements metaphors to portray the liberation of people of African descent in the US and the Islamic concept of Fatiha No. 6. She expresses, “Through airy roads he wings his instant flight/ to purer regions of celestial light” (Wheatley). These lines represent the baby’s journey through “airy roads” and their arrival to Heaven. This could be seen as a metaphor for the enslaved African people and their fight for freedom. The “airy roads” could symbolize the route to liberation Black people took. Additionally, another metaphor that conveys progress in racial equality is “Thrones and dominions cannot tempt me there” (Wheatley). This line can be perceived as a declaration that the desire for power and dominion in the material world is no longer relevant, which means that the oppression system will be repudiated by society and will be replaced by a commitment to morality and ethics.


Additionally, the previous line also represents Fatiha No.6. “The path of those You have blessed” (Harris, 5). Chapter 1, Verse 6. Wheatley transmits that those whose teachings are aligned with their actions will be blessed and guided towards the divine spiritual elevation. Regarding slavery, people who avoid leverage of power and resources as well as dehumanizing temptations will be blessed for adhering to Fatiha No. 4 too. Hence, Wheatley underscores that whereas enslaved people who follow the path of morality will be blessed by Allah, people who proclaim themselves as Christians but support slavery will not transcend to Heaven and receive the blessings of righteousness. Along with these metaphors, Wheatley makes a social commentary of the lack of coherence between professing and practicing Christian values.


Wheatley implements irony in “A Funeral Poem on the Death of C.E. an Infant of Twelve Months,” through a critique of Christian people and their perception of African people. Throughout the poem, she expresses the anguish people of color experienced during the eighteenth century. However, she implements irony by saying that even if Black people are not accepted on Earth by Christians, they will be accepted anywhere by Muslims, especially in Heaven by Allah. She says, “The seat of saints, of seraphs, and of God / Thrice welcome Thou” (Wheatley). Here, Wheatley shows how Allah and his prophets receive Black people with a sense of divine justice and impartiality. On the other hand, these lines imply how ironic and hypocritical many Christian people are. They are supposed to represent God in this world, which means accepting any person no matter their race, nationality, socioeconomic status, or culture. But they do the opposite; through these lines, Wheatley shows the contradictions between Christian principles and the reality of racial inequality. Besides that, she is implementing irony by saying that even though Black people might not enjoy liberty on Earth, they will enjoy infinite freedom in the afterlife and with the almighty God. Thus, by emphasizing the contrast between human biases and the religions’ principles of acceptance and love, Wheatley’s use of irony and other figurative language, such as symbols, makes a strong argument about the actual meaning of Islam and the promise of an equal and better society than the one in the eighteenth century.


Furthermore, Wheatley employs a variety of symbols that represent how Allah and other Muslim representatives support equality and the quest for freedom. Wheatley says, “The angels view him with delight unknown/ Press his soft hand, and seat him on his throne” (Wheatley). In these lines, angels can depict divine intervention in pursuing liberty. During this historical context of slavery, many people from African descent, such as Wheatley demonstrates, convert their fear into powerful faith as a source of strength and optimism. Therefore, the angel’s reception of the dead child can be understood as support for emancipation from slavery. Furthermore, she claims, “Then smilling this: ‘To this divine abode” (Wheatley). These lines come after the ones mentioned previously in order to describe the journey and process to gain freedom. Each line has a specific purpose that conveys how Black people struggle with inhumane conditions but take their conviction as a force to help them be alive to fight for justice, equal opportunities, and liberty. In this interpretation, Heaven symbolizes a world where slavery and oppression do not exist anymore, where people of all backgrounds are accepted and treated equally without any bias as Islam states.


Through “On Being Brought from Africa to America” and “A Funeral Poem on the Death of C. E. an Infant of Twelve Months,” it can be inferred there are many references that can be strongly connected to Islam. Besides the literary analysis provided from a Muslim viewpoint, historical evidence has also demonstrated that many enslaved people were Muslims and that most probable Phillis Wheatley was one of them. More than the religious and historical contexts, Wheatley’s significance transcends these aspects. Phillis Wheatley is the pioneer of African American Literature not only for being the first enslaved woman to write and publish in English but also for being the first one to reconcile her African roots from her past and her new experiences in America.


Further research that compares more poems and their high correlation with Islam will be necessary to make stronger claims about Wheatley’s religious faith. Even though she could have taken some purest ideas of Christianity, that are similar to Islam, this research seeks to demonstrate that it is most likely that Wheatley saw Christian slave traders as the reason she got separated from her family and African roots. Thus, the perception she had of Christian people is associated with the most traumatic moment of her life and the lives of many African enslaved people. Finally, one Muslim symbolic representation might be a coincidence, but more than the multiple Islamic references she portrays, are clear messages that she had encounters with Islam during her first seven years of life in the Senegambia region.
