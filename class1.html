Analyzing Sartre's The Words from a Marxist Feminist Perspective


Daniela Castiblanco Diaz


Dr. Fezzey


ENGL 479


9 May 2024


Whereas women have been prejudged for the sake of their gender, men have enjoyed their privilege of power. Although the focus of most of the historical periods is intertwined with economic development and national power, oppression is evidenced in the most developed societies. The twentieth century faced the highest levels of deprived human rights violations, increasing the rates of inequality in classes and genders. Jean-Paul Sartre, the father of Existentialism, studies the nature of human existence and the sense of living, as a consequence of the Second World War. Sartre presents his self-analysis in his autobiography The Words, where he studies the influence external factors, class privilege, and family have over his beliefs and intellectual strength. Unconsciously, he navigates power dynamics and gender roles during the twentieth century in France, underlining a paradox. While there was an advancement in economy and education, there was a lack of progress in gender equality, highlighting the patriarchal society in Europe. The absence of these two main social aspects is analyzed through the Feminist and Marxist approaches that emphasize the intersectionality between gender and the system of private property. Hence, it is fruitful to analyze The Words from a Marxist Feminist perspective that intertwines the concepts of performative identity and reproductive labor through the analysis of Louis Althusser’s conscious and unconscious ideological apparatuses. Through the two segments of the book called “Reading” and “Writing,” Sartre underscores how genders are obligated to perform in a specific manner and how women are represented and treated in society.In analyzing sexist representations of women in the twentieth century, this paper will demonstrate how Jean-Paul Sartre’s The Words, either consciously or unconsciously, depicts women’s performative identity, reinforcing misogyny through reproductive labor, and increasing social inequality.


In order to provide an accurate analysis of how the Marxist Feminist and psychoanalytical approach is evidenced in Sartre’s The Words, I will juxtapose the themes studied, such as capitalism, gender, societal influences, identity, and the unconscious; as such, I am building on previous scholars’ work to provide a stronger literary analysis from an autobiography that is not explicitly related to the Marxist Feminist theory. Specifically, I will incorporate the works of Louis Althusser, Judith Butler, Marie-Andree Charbonneau, Christian Gilliam, Mukesh Kumar Khatwani, Karl Marx, Thomas Meagher, and Sara Salem. Althusser navigates the effects of “ideological state apparatuses” on individuals’ behaviors, actions, decisions, and overall lives. He studies how societal structures unconsciously shape lived experiences and reproduce social relations of power. Butler develops a post-structuralist concept of feminism known as “performative identity” that analyzes how actions are created through social conventions and power dynamics. She also studies how systems reinforce conceptions of gender and sexuality and thus she is one of this article’s primary resources. Charbonneau draws the similarities and differences between the existentialist philosopher Sartre, and the psychoanalytic theorist Freud. She interconnects the existentialist movement and the psychoanalytic theory by reflecting on human experiences and subjectivity as well as demonstrating how Sartre can be considered Freudian and Freud can be understood as Sartrian. Gilliam presents an existential analysis that underscores how Sartre perceives freedom as the liberty to make decisions that define individuals’ essence. He introduces the dialectic of freedom to study the authenticity human beings do not enjoy. He also highlights the absence of a “pure”existential state due to their freedom and social conditioning limitations. Khatwani presents the findings of a doctoral study that analyzes the correlation between women’s subordination, socioeconomic status, autonomy, and respectability. The dependent variable is misogyny, and the independent variables are income, gender, age, socioeconomic status, family, and opportunities. Khatwani demonstrates how women are exploited and discriminated which can vary from public to private sectors in patriarchal societies. Marx analyzes the unequal distribution of wealth along with commodities, labor, and productive/reproductive labor. He criticizes how capitalism oppresses marginalized groups, including women; the reason why this resource is used as one of my primary resources. Meagher explores the theme of autonomy within the context of human desire. He invokes the realities between the concept of existential psychoanalysis and the notion of sociogeny and how these sociogenic dimensions can strengthen dominant ideologies. Salem studies the use of the concept of “intersectionality” in feminist scholarly articles as well as Marxist Feminism. Her theoretical work analyzes the trajectory and impact of intersectionality in the beliefs of Marxist Feminism. Now that I have established a review of the scholarship of the topics, I will start my analysis of how Marxist Feminism is portrayed in The Words.


Sartre studies the impact social factors such as high education and family, consciously and unconsciously, have had on him. He emphasizes the evolution of his family and the pattern genders consciously and unconsciously follow, transcending to new generations. By displaying the role of his parents, he depicts how genders are not allowed to behave freely without experiencing any social pressure. Sartre expresses, “There is no good father, that’s the rule. Don’t lay the blame on men but on the bond of paternity, which is rotten” (19). This statement implies that fathers are obligated to imitate what is considered to behave like a father, which in the long-term would be performed naturally due to the repetition of the same act. In this example, the act of being a good father is prohibited because that could be associated withsensibility. This statement is also a metaphor that compares the bond of paternity with something that is deteriorated, embodying how dysfunctional paternity can be. Similarly, Sartre highlights his mother’s oppressed actions. He says, “Following her mother’s example, my mother preferred duty to pleasure” (16). This simile shows the system’s intent to maintain a conservative idea of what men and women are expected to be. Sartre compares his parents’ behaviors with previous generations and the French twentieth century. These parental/gender cases exemplify Judith Butler’s concept of performative identity, which drives the conclusion that individuals are not consciously able to discover and decide their own identity due to the influence society has over them.


Butler, a feminist theorist, develops the concept of performative identity by studying how society forms individual identities through repeated performances. She examines to what extent gender is socially constructed or inherited. Butler engages in this study to determine whether gender exists or if behaviors are associated with gender and social expectations. She states, “Does being female constitute a ‘natural fact’ or a cultural performance, or is ‘naturalness’ constituted through discursively constrained performative acts that produce the body through and within the categories of sex” (2541). This proposition emphasizes the question of how cultural performance has become natural because of the constant repetition of actions and words influenced by powerful and patriarchal societies. Her theoretical approach can be applied to literary works that delve into gender performances, such as Sartre’s The Words, as well as writings intersected with theories that compare the origins of gender inequality and oppression such as the Marxist with its concept of productive and reproductive labor.


Karl Marx develops an economic and social theory, stipulating an inclusive and socialist system that benefits every member of the community by prioritizing their human rights. He criticizes capitalism, its affections over classes and gender, and the unequal types of labor. Marxdivides labor into productive and reproductive, creating a system where the value of goods and services is measured by labor instead of personal economic desires for capital, as well as a system that also values unpaid and intangible work. Productive labor is the production of commodities or services that involve economic values, where the majority of the income goes to the bourgeoise (owner) and the other fraction to the proletariat (working class). On the other hand, reproductive labor is responsible for everyday occupations and sustaining human life by cultivating and raising future generations; a job that is principally assigned to women, possibly influenced by Social Darwinism conceptions. Even though this work is unpaid, it has invaluable significance for the family and transcendence over society. Marx argues, “The value of labour-power was determined, not only by the labour-time necessary to maintain the individual adult labourer, but also by that necessary to maintain his family” (271). This premise argues how labor power goes beyond individual productivity and productive labor, to value the support of the worker’s family unit, and cease social and gender inequality.


This Marxist theory can be highly compared to Sartre’s premises in The Words. Sartre underlines his lack of respect for his mother due to her low social standing. His disrespect lies in the fact that no one around him treats his mother humanely. Sartre states, “How can I respect her if no one else does” (21). Sartre uses this rhetorical question to illustrate how women are often undervalued in society due to their reproductive work, which includes cooking, cleaning, and raising children, causing oppression and thus misogynistic behaviors, primarily in men. Sartre believes that if no one respects his mother, why would he? If his mother would contribute to any progress in his family, especially monetary, he would value her differently. This means that for a person to be considered to a high standard, they must have paid work that visibly improves people’s lives. As Renault analyzes types of labor within the capitalist system, he highlights that,


Insofar as these different labor processes are necessary for capitalist production, as Marx argues, and capitalists benefit from them, while this labor remains unpaid it is tempting to conclude that this is exploited labor. Insofar as it is women who are generally assigned the labor of the reproduction of labor-power, this then leads to the idea that the capitalist exploitation of productive labor is accompanied by the capitalist exploitation of reproductive labor assigned to women(47).


Renault’s analysis concludes that the exploitation of reproductive laborers, who are mostly women, is the result of the initial abuse of productive labor, which is predominantly done by men. This second abuse arises from the unequal distribution of wealth, where the bourgeoisie takes advantage of their workers by taking most of the profit while paying them miserable wages. This socioeconomic injustice obligates the proletariat to work extra hours and thus have less contribution to their households. The lack of support from men within the family creates two main beliefs: firstly, that men are more valuable for contributing economically to the family, and secondly, that the mistreatment of women is natural. In general, Renault’s insights underscore a strong connection between Marxism and Feminism, which can be exemplified in Sartre’s mother’s reproductive labor. Anne Marie’s lack of value in society due to her unemployment led her to change her professional path. Sartre writes,


Without money or a profession, Anne Marie decided to live with her parents. But my father’s insolent decease had displeased the Schewizters; it looked too much like a repudiation. My mother was deemed guilty of not having foreseen or forestalled it… To obtain forgiveness, she gave herself unstintingly, kept the house for her parents in Meudon and then in Paris, became nurse, majordomo, companion, servant. (17)


This vivid description shows how Sartre’s mother had to find a paid job to improve her social standing and gain respect, illustrating the intersection of economic and social systems on gender inequality. Sartre’s word choice is strong, especially with the terms: “insolent,” “repudiation,” and “unstintingly,” producing a negative perception of his family and French society in the twentieth century. These words portray societal expectations men and women had to meet to be accepted and recognized within their familiar lineage. The overall recounting of his mother’s life path, constitutes the roots of women’s inequality within capitalism and its productive and reproductive labor, developing a new concept of Marxist feminism.


As Sartre delves into power dynamics and gender roles in the twentieth century French society, a question arises: Do patriarchal and misogynistic societies originate because of capitalism and the repetition of women’s actions1 within the reproductive labor, or because of cultural and historical norms that existed before capitalism that normalize the marginalization of the feminine sex? This rhetorical question involves the principles of the Marxist feminist approach. Salem expresses,


In order to understand exploitation, and connections between different forms of exploitation, we need to locate these within capitalism—and this is what Marxist feminism does. Marxist feminism offers a means through which we can explain why different categories intersect and how they came about in the first place. (410)


Salem’s proposition suggests that analyzing oppression from a multidisciplinary viewpoint provides accurate apprehensions on how different ideologies coexist and intertwine, resulting in similar struggles and outcomes: powerless social classes experience exploitation within both labor and gender contexts. Along the same line, Khatwani integrates capitalism, patriarchy, and women’s oppression to explain how the subordination of women either emerged alongside private property or thrived from capitalist exploitation and inequality. Her studies question whether mainly develops from patriarchy or capitalism. She argues that capitalism and patriarchy are interdependent systems that must be studied together to understand how socioeconomic structures produce control over resources and women. Khatwani states


Marxist feminists believe that both subordination of women and division of classes developed historically with the development of private property…Thus, Marxist feminists believe that abolishment of capitalism is abolishment of patriarchy. According to them women’s oppression performs for capitalism in three different ways (i) reproducing labor force, (ii) absorbing anger the proletariat (workers), and (iii) being reserve army for cheap labor. (8)


Her proposition reiterates how oppression favors capitalism in multiple ways. While women mostly perform reproductive labor, they spend more time on unpaid tasks, facing limitations on employment opportunities to contribute economically to their families and enjoy more independence. Besides struggling to be valued and recognized, women are also expected to provide care and support to the proletariat, that are mainly their husbands (men) who have been exploited within the productive labor. Women have the pressure to play the role of perfect mothers, wives, and even psychologists to prevent challenges against the capitalist system. The aforementioned actions are primarily focused on men, causing women to be isolated from their independence, life, and empowerment. Lastly, Khatwani correlates Marxism and Feminism by underscoring how women are abused by capitalists when needed, especially when facing economic crisis and thus requiring cheap labor. This precise analysis of how the Marxist and Feminist theories are perceived in Sartre’s The Words is also interconnected to the psychology behind Sartre’s conception of women.


Therefore, the Marxist feminist approach can be studied from Althusser’s psychoanalytical lens to analyze the conscious and unconscious impact social factors and social pressures have on Sartre’s standards. Since in The Words, Sartre describes the role of his family and society in his daily routine, it is pertinent to say that societal factors are in strong juxtaposition with his beliefs and philosophical convictions. Sartre says,


I was a fief of the sun, my grandfather could enjoy me without possessing me. I was his “wonder” because he wanted to finish his life as a wonderstruck old man. He chose to regard me as a singular favor of fate, as a gracious and always revocable gift. What could he have required of me? (23)


Sartre’s statement portrays his close bond with his grandfather since he played the role of father and grandfather due to his father’s death. He encapsulates his sense of dependency on his grandfather, through the metaphor “I was a fief of the sun.” It compares Sartre with a sphere that belongs to the sun, demonstrating two main aspects. The first one is that Sartre compares his grandfather with a massive star: the sun, which shows his adoration and devotion for him, almost as if he was a god. The second one is that Sartre is like a celestial body that is subject to the gravitational force of his grandfather’s influence, which in this realistic case is the unconscious assimilation Sartre has of his grandfather’s values. Moreover, Sartre’s acknowledgment, “My grandfather could enjoy me without possessing me,” shows in depth how his grandfather influences him inherently without having the intention to control him directly, as the sun irradiates energy but without possessing the planets. The next lines, “I was his “wonder” because he wanted to finish his life as a wonderstruck old man,” convey the indispensable role Sartre’s grandfather sustains in his life, as planets need the sun for their existence. Sartre’s grandfather’s role transcended from the family duty of being a regular grandfather to fostering Sartre’s intellectual and philosophical progress. Moreover, Sartre implements a figurative language of imagery to paint a vivid picture of his grandfather’s fate. Sartre expresses, “A singular favor of fate, as a gracious and always irrevocable gift” to portray how his grandfather perceives him as a special occurrence that happened to him, almost like a gift from destiny. However, the word “revocable” conveys the potential for changes in their dynamic as Sartre evolves with his philosophy and personal decisions. The last line, “What could he have required of me?” raises two considerations. The first one relates to Freud’s psychoanalytical perception that there will perpetually remain internal pressure from social factors. Although Sartre’s grandfather contributed without any explicit expectation, it can be said that due to his professional career (philosophy), there was an implicit expectation for similar performances or beliefs. The second one is associated with Althusser’s ideological apparatuses and their unconscious influence on individuals; an aspect that is also examined by one scholar. Gilliam states, “The limit of freedom as guaranteed by the unconscious ensures that we are never quite completely rid of all social conditioning, that we never quite reach a ‘pure’ existential state, or a pure negation (359). This argument further supports the point that Sartre unconsciously possesses a strong influence from his grandfather. Although Sartre can change some of his beliefs and philosophical views, his connection with his grandfather is inextricable and to one extent unconscious. Therefore, the reiteration of the impact his grandfather holds on him, as it is perceived in this statement, emphasizes the importance the grandfather has on Sartre’s personal and professional convictions. This statement also demonstrates the effects family, and external factors have on individual identities, as Althusser’s theory proposes.


According to Althusser, ideas are represented in human actions strongly influenced by societal structural factors. This means that ideologies are not only products of people’s conscious selections but are immersed in their everyday actions and interactions with multiple ideological apparatuses. Althusser defines these apparatuses as “civil institutions that have legal standing” such as schools, churches, and families (1333). Althusser says,


The individual in question behaves in such and such a way, adopts such and such a practical attitude, and what is more, participates in certain regular practices which are those of the ideological apparatus on which ‘depend’ the ideas which he has in all consciousness freely chosen as a subject. (1353)


Two theses can be developed from the previous argument concerning the correlation between external factors and Sartre’s essence. The first one is that Sartre consciously adopts many ideas from this ideological apparatus (family and higher education) without questioning the negative repercussions of gender identity and thus misogyny. This thesis is highly intertwined with Butler’s conception of performative identity. Because of the repetition of misogynistic actions in Sartre’s surroundings, the actions and beliefs become natural and thus part of his unconscious. The second one is that the set of ideas/ beliefs of individuals surrounding him, unconsciously makes him a “subject” of the dominant social order. This second hypothesis is strongly interconnected with the psychoanalytical approach studied by Jacques Lacan about how individuals have an imaginary relationship to their real conditions of existence. It is also related to Freud’s theory of how childhood experiences and family dynamics unconsciously influence and put pressure on people’s actions. Therefore, it is pertinent to mention that these two hypotheses could be the result of the ideological apparatus of Sartre’s family. Losing his father, being a philosopher’s grandson, and having access to higher education that was dominantly attended by men, also imposes an implicit social pressure to follow a similar ideological posture to the twentieth-French society, which produces an unconscious familiar influence over his perception of women. Similarly, Meagher studies the correlation between Althusser’s, Freud’s, and Sartre’s visions of how factors such as family influence and determine people’s actions. He says,


For a relational metaphysics, the act can be understood in terms of further relations. An act is only intelligible in connection with the plethora of relations that constitute it. Both Sartre and Freud offer a relational concept of acts and the meanings that constitute them. The difference, though, is that Freud’s conception of humanity and its desires introduces what Sartre terms a ‘vertical determinism,’ for which ‘the history of the subject will decide whether this or that drive will be fixed on this or that object.” (3)


Meagher indicates that as for Althusser the “ideological apparatuses” influence actions and decisions, for Freud and Sartre “vertical determinism” is how people’s history deterministically impacts their desires and actions. Meagher highlights that even though Sartre mostly delves into philosophical dilemmas, he also analyzes individuals’ essence. Hence, it can be argued that the three of them (Althusser, Freud, and Sartre) believe that individuals’ decisions are more than their conscious selections, they are relational and thus strongly shaped by social factors and unconscious pressures, including family dynamics.


As Sartre demonstrates through the two segments of the book “Reading” and “Writing,” his behaviors and personal ideologies regarding women are most likely to be socially constructed instead of inherent. Being raised in a society where men are predominantly more valued and intellectually respected suggests that Sartre’s misogynistic behaviors are largely unconscious due to the constant repetition of actions, thus not perceived as negative. This analysis develops a strong intersection of theories: Marxism, Feminism, and Psychoanalytical, demonstrating the necessity for more multidisciplinary approaches for more objective conclusions.


Further research on three different fields will contribute to the Marxist feminist theory and its correlation with the psychoanalytical approach. The first inquiry concerns historical evidence on women’s inequality in French society during the twentieth century, which will provide new considerations on to what extent the society, his family, or himself influenced Sartre’s vision of women. The second question examines the study of Social Darwinism and the belief that society should operate like a “survival of the fittest” competition, where only the strongest and most successful individuals should survive and thrive in life. The analysis of how Social Darwinism promoted women to contribute primarily to reproductive rather than productive labor would bring a deeper understanding of the roots of misogyny. Finally, incorporating the work of Lacan and the imaginary relationships would contribute to the research on the psychology behind Sartre and the overall influence on Existentialism, the predominant philosophy in the twentieth century.
